    Matching and designs¶

    Selecting stimuli is the part of an experiment that most often decides what the experiment can show, and it is the part that is least often written down. This guide follows a set from the corpus to the finished selection: how a lexicon becomes a candidate pool, which dimensions can be equated, what the four matching methods actually do, how to read the report that says whether the matching worked, and what to do instead when dichotomising a predictor is the wrong move.

    The examples run against the lexicon bundled with the package, so you can paste them into a session and get the output shown. Only the norm-merging sketch needs a file of your own.

    from importlib.resources import files

import yaml

import lexsync

data = files("lexsync") / "data"
schema = yaml.safe_load((data / "schema.yaml").read_text(encoding="utf-8"))
lexicon = lexsync.load_lexicon(
    str(data / "en_example.csv"), schema, language="english"
)

    From lexicon to pool¶

    load_lexicon reads a derived corpus and does rather more than parse a CSV. It checks the column contract from the schema, which requires word and freq_zipf and nothing else. It drops rows with a missing word or frequency, lower-cases and strips the rest, and removes duplicates. Then it sorts the whole table by the UTF-8 bytes of the word and numbers the rows from one.

    That sort is the quiet step everything else depends on. Byte order is stable where a platform's collation is not, so the lexicon comes out in the same order on a German laptop and an English continuous integration runner, and in the same order as the R engine, which sorts the same way. Everything downstream inherits it, including the id column, which is the last tie-break in the matcher.

    Three columns are derived on the way in: length in characters, n_syllables from vowel runs, and frequency, which is whatever column the schema's dimensions.frequency.column names, by default freq_zipf.

    print(lexicon.head(3).to_string(index=False))

     word  freq_zipf language   source  n_density  old20  id  length  n_syllables  frequency
  aaa       3.77  english wordfreq          7   1.65   1       3            1       3.77
  aba       3.32  english wordfreq          9   1.40   2       3            2       3.32
aback       2.99  english wordfreq          0   1.95   3       5            2       2.99

    load_lexicon takes the lexicon as given and screens nothing for lexical status. The bundled English slice loaded here is cut from the screened English lexicon. The screen leaves out the forms SUBTLEX-US lacks or writes with a capital in more than 90% of their uses, such as proper names and acronyms. The slice's N and OLD20 count only the forms it keeps. Taboo words and some abbreviations pass the screen, and a lexicon of your own is loaded as it stands. Choosing and screening a lexicon below says what such forms do to a selection and how to screen them out.

    build_pool narrows that lexicon to the candidates a design will consider. A two-element numeric range is read as an inclusive [min, max] band, and anything else, [true, false] included, is read as a set of allowed values. A set of numbers is compared with a numeric column as numbers. Any other set is compared as text, the cells and the values written as the R engine writes them, so true matches TRUE in a logical column and never 1 in a 0/1 column. Rows missing the filtered column are dropped, and a filter naming a column the lexicon does not have is skipped without complaint.

    pool = lexsync.build_pool(lexicon, {"length": [3, 8], "frequency": [3.8, 7.0]})
print(len(pool))

    1035

    The pool step is not optional even though nothing enforces it. match_stimuli never looks at a design's pool_filters. It matches over whatever frame you hand it. A script that skips build_pool therefore matches over the entire lexicon and silently ignores the design's bands, and the selection will look plausible while answering a different question. The package's own test suite pins this, checking that the README's example calls build_pool before matching.

    Choosing and screening a lexicon¶

    The lexica in the repository's corpora/derived/ hold the most frequent forms wordfreq gives for a language, and wordfreq lists names, acronyms and internet forms beside words. Such forms enter a selection unevenly across conditions: when the en_andrews_repro demonstration read en.csv, they made up 24 of its 36 low-frequency items and none of its 36 high-frequency ones. N and OLD20 count them as neighbours too, so that cat has 32 neighbours in en.csv, among them caa, cbt and cdt.

    corpora/derived/en_v2.csv screens the English lexicon against SUBTLEX-US (Brysbaert & New, 2009). It holds the 30,000 forms and frequencies of en.csv and adds four columns. in_subtlex_us is 1 when SUBTLEX-US lists the form, and lowercase_share is the share of its SUBTLEX-US uses that begin with a lower-case letter, empty for a form SUBTLEX-US lacks. nonstandard is 1 for a form SUBTLEX-US lacks or whose share is below 0.10, which marks 6,565 forms, mostly names and acronyms, and reference_member is 1 for the other 23,435. The file's n_density and old20 count neighbours among those only, so cat has 21, and n_density_raw and old20_raw keep the values of en.csv. To select among the unmarked forms, filter on the flag.

    lexicon: corpora/derived/en_v2.csv
pool_filters:
  nonstandard: [0, 0]

    The threshold is low on purpose. A capital at the start of a sentence pulls ordinary words down, so even what, how, why, maybe, okay and yes begin with a lower-case letter in under half their uses. A filter such as lowercase_share: [0.5, 1] would drop them all. Even at 0.10 the flag marks the days of the week, the months other than march and may, most nationality words, god, ok and tv. It also marks every form the 2009 corpus lacks, such as selfie, emoji and covid. A design that needs some of those can leave the flag unfiltered and remove what it does not want by name. A range filter on lowercase_share would also drop every form SUBTLEX-US lacks, whose share is empty.

    exclude_words removes words by name. It lists them in the design, or names a UTF-8 file with one word per line, in which blank lines and lines beginning # are skipped.

    exclude_words: [adams, dubai, espn]
# or, from a file: exclude_words: {path: config/exclude_en.txt}

    The words are trimmed, lower-cased and put in NFC, as a lexicon's words are. They leave the candidates of a corpus, pool or generated design after any norms: join and before the pool filters. The neighbourhood reference still counts them, and a generated pseudoword still may not equal one, because an excluded word is still a word of the language. A design with an item table refuses the key, since its items are already a selection of your own. The datasheet records the list as materials_source.exclusions, with its checksum, the number of words it lists and the number of candidates it removed, and the Methods paragraph reports the removal.

    en.csv keeps its bytes, so a design written for lexsync 0.1 selects what it selected. The English demonstrations read en_v2.csv with the filter above. Their exclude_words file, config/exclude_en_demo.txt, leaves out the taboo words and the forms that pass the screen without being standard English words, such as abbreviations that subtitles write in lower case (mph, msg). Its header states the criteria, and the list was made by reading what the demonstrations selected. Spanish and Chinese have no screened lexicon, because no source of letter case or known words with a verified licence exists for them. Read a selection in either language before a study runs, and leave out what you find with exclude_words.

    The datasheet credits the lexicon a corpus or generated design read. A lexicon lexsync ships is credited to wordfreq, and en_v2.csv and the English example slice drawn from it to wordfreq screened against SUBTLEX-US, with the rule. A corpus that fetch_corpus put in the cache is credited by the registry's citation, and any other file by its name and the first twelve digits of its checksum.

    Supplied item pools¶

    Narrowing a whole lexicon is the right way round when any word of the language will do. It is the wrong way round when the candidate set is itself a research decision: a list from a previous study, from a norming session, or one restricted to a semantic category that no lexical filter can express. Such a list used to have to masquerade as a lexicon, which meant inventing a freq_zipf column for it, or else skip matching entirely by going in as items.source: table.

    items.source: pool takes the list as it is.

    items:
  source: pool
  path: items/pool_en_concrete_nouns.csv
  lexicon: corpora/derived/en_v2.csv

    The list needs only a word column. Length and the syllable estimate are derived from the form, and items.lexicon supplies the rest, frequency above all, by lookup. A word the lexicon does not have raises a hard error, because the tolerance windows drop rows with missing values silently and the pool would then be smaller than it appears to be.

    One subtlety would quietly invalidate the controls if it went the other way. n_density and old20 are properties of a word in its language, not among the 131 words of a supplied list, so they are computed against the lexicon's words. A supplied pool with no lexicon falls back to itself, which is why one is given above. load_pool returns both parts of that, {"pool": ..., "reference": ...}, if you call it directly outside a design.

    Everything downstream is unchanged: the same conditions, the same match_on, the same report, the same datasheet. What differs is only where the candidates came from, and the datasheet records the list's checksum so that is answerable later. config/design_en_supplied_pool.yaml is a worked example.

    Relational designs, where the item is a pair¶

    A priming study's theoretical variable is a property of the pair, such as distributional similarity or associative strength, while its nuisance variables are properties of each member: frequency, length, neighbourhood. A design could previously have matching, or its own item table, but not both, so a relational design had to be assembled by hand.

    items.members promotes an ordinary item table to a pair-keyed one.

    items:
  source: table
  path: items/priming_pairs_en.csv
  members: [prime, target]             # the two word fields
  lexicon: corpora/derived/en_v2.csv   # where each member's norms are looked up
  anchor_condition: related            # whose row represents the pair during selection

    Each member's word-level norms are joined from the lexicon and prefixed, giving prime.frequency, target.length and so on. The member name leads and the dimension follows, for a reason specific to R: prime.frequency is safe because R's df$prime still exact-matches the bare prime column, whereas frequency.prime would be a partial-matching hazard. Those prefixed names are then usable wherever a dimension is: in pool_filters, in match_on, as a continuous.predictor or as a control.

    pair.lev is the Levenshtein distance between the two members and pair.overlap is 1 - pair.lev / length of the longer form, a normalised orthographic similarity. It scores edit operations, so it is not a count of shared material: abc and cba share every letter but score 0.33. Orthographic overlap is the standard confound control in a priming design, since a related pair that also shares letters confounds semantic relatedness with orthographic similarity. The pipeline adds both automatically when the two members are named prime and target. Under any other member names, call add_pair_overlap on the item table yourself. A predictor that cannot be computed from the forms alone, such as cosine similarity or forward associative strength, arrives as an extra column on the item table and is used like any other.

    n_per_condition: 8                  # counts PAIRS: 8 pairs, 16 rows, 8 per list over 2 lists
pool_filters:
  target.length: [3, 7]
  target.frequency: [3.0, 6.0]
continuous:
  predictor: target.frequency
  controls: [target.length, pair.overlap]
match_on: [target.length, pair.overlap]

    Two rules follow from the item being a pair. A filter applies to a pair only if every one of its rows passes, and selection runs over one row per pair, the anchor_condition, defaulting to the byte-first condition, with the result re-expanded afterwards so every condition row of every chosen pair survives. Neither is an optimisation. A filter on prime.frequency applied row by row would keep a pair's related row and drop its unrelated one, leaving a set the Latin-square counterbalancer cannot complete. Only a dimension that varies within the pair can split it that way, which the prime-level and pair-level ones do and the target-level ones do not, since the target is the same word in both rows.

    A member name may not be one of word, id, set, list, trial, condition, replicate or item, which the engines read directly, and a design using one is refused. The datasheet gains a relational block recording the members, the pair count, the member lexicon and its checksum, and the member-level dimensions separately from the relational ones. config/design_en_priming_continuous.yaml is the worked example, and its own comments go further into why each rule is there.

    The dimensions¶

    Six dimensions are declared in the schema. Three arrive from load_lexicon: length and n_syllables derived from the form, frequency read from the column the schema names. n_density and old20 are carried by every corpus lexsync distributes, so they too are usually present at load time, and add_neighbourhood computes them only for a lexicon that lacks them. bigram_freq is the one dimension that always needs a separate call.

    Dimension Unit Where it comes from
    length letters Derived at load time from the word.
    frequency Zipf The lexicon's freq_zipf column (van Heuven et al., 2014).
    n_density neighbours add_neighbourhood: Coltheart's N, same-length single substitutions (Coltheart et al., 1977).
    old20 mean Levenshtein distance add_neighbourhood: the mean distance to the 20 nearest words (Yarkoni et al., 2008).
    n_syllables syllables Derived at load time by counting maximal vowel runs, and missing outside the Latin script.
    bigram_freq mean bigram probability (type-based, non-positional) add_bigram_frequency, a phonotactic-probability proxy.

    add_neighbourhood computes both neighbourhood measures against a reference word list, which should normally be the full lexicon, since a word's neighbours do not stop existing because your design excluded them. The pipeline passes the full lexicon for exactly that reason. It computes n_density, old20 and bigram_freq for a lexicon that lacks them whenever the design refers to them, whether in match_on, a condition's define_by, pool_filters, a continuous predictor or control, or counterbalance.balance_on. The filters the lexicon can answer run first and those on a derived dimension after it is computed, so the calculation, quadratic in the reference size, runs on the smaller pool. A lexicon can name the words that make up the reference with a reference_member column (1 for a member, 0 otherwise). Passing reference_words to run_pipeline recomputes N and OLD20, and bigram_freq where the lexicon carries it or the design uses it, against that list instead, overriding the lexicon's columns. The datasheet's selection.neighbourhood_reference then records the list with the columns it was applied to.

    add_bigram_frequency averages, over a word's adjacent letter bigrams, the corpus probability of each. It works from integer counts and rounds the result to nine decimal places, which keeps it identical in the two engines to the last decimal place.

    count_syllables is honest about being an estimate. It counts maximal runs of Latin vowels, an orthographic approximation with no pronunciation model behind it.

    print(
    [lexsync.count_syllables(w) for w in ["cat", "banana", "strength", "idea"]]
)

    [1, 3, 1, 2]

    strength returning 1 and idea returning 2 shows both the method and its limits in one line.

    A vowel counts whatever its accent, since the word is decomposed and its diacritics set aside, so Polish będę, Turkish kız, Czech dům and Vietnamese một count 2, 1, 1 and 1. The estimate is defined for Latin orthographies only: a word holding a character outside the Latin script returns None, and its n_syllables is NaN. run_pipeline refuses a design that refers to n_syllables when such a word passes the other filters the lexicon can answer. Every Chinese, Russian or Greek word used to count 0 syllables, and a design matched on them reported perfect balance. Reproducibility and parity gives the exact letters and code points.

    The set of dimensions is not closed. merge_norms left-joins any word-keyed norm table, which is the connector for anything the corpus does not carry: concreteness, age of acquisition, valence, or the English Lexicon Project's behavioural measures. The norm data are fetched separately, because their licensing varies, and merged here so the matcher can equate on the new column as if it had been there all along.

    # illustrative: needs a norm table of your own at norms/concreteness_en.csv
lexicon = lexsync.merge_norms(lexicon, "norms/concreteness_en.csv", on="word",
                              columns=["concreteness"])
# then: match_on: [length, frequency, concreteness]

    The join drops missing keys, takes the first row per key, and is case- and whitespace-insensitive on both sides. The result is the lexicon itself with columns appended, in its own row and column order, which is what makes the two engines agree by construction: pandas.merge and R's merge() disagree about where the key column lands and about how they disambiguate a name clash. A clash is refused, because a silent rename leaves the dimension the design matches on under a name nothing looks for.

    In a design you do not call this yourself. Name the tables in a norms: block and the pipeline joins them before the pool is built, so a norm column can be filtered on, matched on or spanned like any other dimension.

    norms:
  - path: norms/concreteness_en.csv
    join_on: word             # optional join key, default 'word'
    columns: [concreteness]   # optional; default every column but the key

pool_filters:
  concreteness: [2.0, 5.0]
match_on: [length, concreteness]

    The join key is written join_on. This page used to write it on:, which YAML reads as the boolean true, so the join used word whatever the entry named. A bare on: is now read as the join key, with a note in the run log, and any other key in an entry is refused.

    lexsync ships no norm data: licences vary, and the citation is yours to honour. What it does do is record what you joined. Every table appears in the materials datasheet with its checksum and its per-column coverage, because a norm table can supply the very variable a design manipulates, and a selection over columns of unstated origin is not reproducible from the record that exists to make it so. Coverage is recorded for a related reason: a word the table does not cover gets a missing value. A filter on the column drops it, and the matcher excludes it before selecting whenever the column is a matched dimension, a continuous predictor or a control. The run log and the datasheet count those exclusions per condition.

    How matching works¶

    match_stimuli runs in two stages, and understanding the split explains most of its behaviour. Both need at least one dimension in match_on. Without one, every candidate lies at distance zero from every anchor and the tie-break alone would choose the words, so a design with conditions and no match_on, or an empty one, is refused before anything is selected.

    First it standardises. Every matched dimension is z-scored against the whole pool's mean and sample standard deviation, so a distance in letters and a distance in Zipf units become comparable. A dimension with zero or undefined spread gets a scale of 1, which keeps the distance finite. A candidate missing any matched dimension has no place in that space, so every condition's subpool is cut to its complete rows before any method runs. The run log and the datasheet count what each condition lost, and the shortfall policy below applies to what remains.

    Then it picks an anchor. The first condition in the design is the anchor, and its subpool is sorted by the first dimension its define_by mentions, with a byte-order tie-break, after which the selection is an even spread across that sorted subpool. Taking the top n would pile the anchor into one corner of its own band. The even spread makes the anchor representative of the condition it is meant to define.

    The anchor's realised mean and standard deviation on each matched dimension then set a tolerance window, mean ± k × SD, where k comes from matching.tolerance_k in the schema and may be overridden per dimension by the design. This is the anchored path, so it is what standardised_euclidean and mahalanobis do. joint and optimal score cross-condition pairs directly and never read tolerance_k at all. The defaults are 2.0 for length, n_density and old20, and 1.0 for frequency, and any other dimension takes 2. The design's values are merged over the schema's as R's modifyList() merges two lists. A null in the schema is ignored, and a null in the design removes the schema's value for that dimension, so either leaves k at 2. Each remaining condition is filtered to its window, and then every anchor item is assigned its nearest unused candidate by standardised distance.

    The assignment is where determinism is bought. Distances are rounded to nine decimal places before they are compared, which absorbs last-bit floating-point differences between the two engines. Each anchor item chooses among the candidates not yet used, so no word can be assigned twice. A tie in distance is broken by the rule that matching.tie_break names, and then by the lexicon id.

    Exact ties are common, because lengths and neighbourhood counts are whole numbers and frequencies carry two decimals. When en_freqcontrast read en.csv, 71 of its 80 anchored picks had another candidate at exactly the chosen distance, with a median of eight tied candidates, and the tie-break chose the word. Under bytes, the rule of lexsync 0.1, a tie goes to the word whose UTF-8 bytes sort first, so a condition decided by ties fills with early-alphabet words. In that design, 61% of the low-frequency words began with a or b, against 13% of their candidates, and in en_ndensity's dense condition every word did. A word's initial phoneme biases voice-key measures of naming latency (Kessler et al., 2002), and the realised-control report cannot show such a skew, because it measures only the matched dimensions.

    Under hash, the default, a tie goes to the smaller SHA-256 digest of lexsync-tie-v1| followed by the word. The salt is fixed, so the rule does not depend on the seed, and the digests favour no part of the alphabet. On en.csv, the low-frequency share fell to 12.5% and the dense share to 17.5%, against 13% and 12% among the candidates, while Cohen's d on every matched dimension of every shipped matched design was unchanged. Under either rule, standardised_euclidean and joint give the same selection in the two engines, and bytes reproduces the selections of lexsync 0.1. The even spreads that pick the anchor's items and a continuous design's items keep the byte order under both rules.

    matching:
  tie_break: bytes   # hash (the default) or bytes, in a design or in the schema

    Two guards are worth knowing about because they turn silent corruption into an error. If a condition has fewer candidates inside its window than the anchor has items, the window is relaxed to the whole subpool, and verbose=True says so. If even the relaxed subpool is too small, match_stimuli raises. Without that check, an exhausted pool would leave an anchor item with no unused candidate to take.

    Both behaviours are policies, and the schema's matching block names them. on_insufficient_tolerance governs the window fallback. Its default of relax keeps the behaviour just described, while error makes the matcher refuse and say so, which is the honest setting when the window is itself the criterion, as it is when reproducing a published study's stated windows. shortfall governs what happens when fewer sets than n_per_condition can be selected at all, and it defaults to error deliberately, because a smaller set changes the design that was planned. A design that can live with fewer sets says so with shortfall: allow. The datasheet then records the items each condition holds, and the methods paragraph states them beside the request. Either key may be overridden under the design's own matching: block, exactly as tolerance_k may be.

    The four methods¶

    The method is set with matching.method in a design, or globally in the schema.

    Method What it does Conditions Cross-engine
    standardised_euclidean Greedy nearest neighbour on z-scored dimensions, anchored on the first condition. The default. Any number Byte-identical
    joint Scores every cross-condition pair and greedily takes the cheapest disjoint ones. Exactly two Byte-identical
    mahalanobis Nearest neighbour under a covariance-aware distance that down-weights correlated dimensions. Any number Equivalent, byte-identity not guaranteed
    optimal Selects the n pairs of least summed distance, solved exactly as an assignment problem. Exactly two Equivalent, byte-identity not guaranteed

    joint differs from the default in what it is allowed to discard. The anchored matcher fixes the first condition and then finds the best counterpart for each of its items, so a bad anchor item drags a bad pair into the set. joint keeps only items that have a good counterpart, which matters when the manipulation is confounded with a control, neighbourhood density with word length being the standard case. Both conditions are first capped to the 1200 rows nearest the other condition's centroid, which keeps the all-pairs cost matrix tractable, and the cap itself is applied to rounded distances. Pairs of equal cost, and rows tied at the cap, are ordered by the same tie_break rule as the anchored picks.

    mahalanobis uses the inverse of the pool's correlation matrix in standardised space as its metric, with a small ridge to survive near-collinear dimensions, so that two dimensions measuring much the same thing are not counted twice (Rubin, 1980; Stuart, 2010). The correlations come from the pool's rows that are complete on every matched dimension, and with k matched dimensions the pool needs at least k + 2 such rows. optimal selects the n disjoint pairs whose summed distance is smallest. Choosing n pairs from two pools is the k-cardinality assignment problem, and lexsync solves it exactly by padding the cost matrix with dummy rows and columns until every optimal assignment uses exactly n real pairs (Volgenant, 2004). Greedy joint matching can spend a hard item's only good counterpart on an item that had alternatives, and optimal cannot, although on the pools tested joint came within a few per cent of the optimum. When the conditions overlap, a word cannot be paired with itself or used twice. A pair lost that way is replaced by solving again over the unused words, and the selection is then no longer guaranteed to have the least summed distance. Gu and Rosenbaum (1993) compared optimal and greedy pair matching of every treated unit, a related problem, and found optimal matching no better at balancing the covariates.

    Both of these carry the parity caveat. A matrix inverse and an assignment solver are the two places where the R and Python linear-algebra backends can disagree in their last bits, and an assignment solver's tie handling differs outright. The engines agree closely, and mahalanobis usually agrees exactly, but neither is guaranteed byte-for-byte. optimal in particular tends to select an equally optimal but different set. Each run's datasheet records which case applies.

    Comparing them on the same pool shows how little separates them when the pool is generous:

    pool = lexsync.build_pool(lexicon, {"length": [4, 8], "frequency": [3.5, 7.0]})
design = {
    "name": "methods", "language": "english", "n_per_condition": 40,
    "conditions": [
        {"name": "high_frequency", "define_by": {"frequency": [5.2, 7.0]}},
        {"name": "low_frequency", "define_by": {"frequency": [3.5, 4.4]}},
    ],
    "match_on": ["length", "n_density", "old20"],
}

for method in ["standardised_euclidean", "joint", "mahalanobis", "optimal"]:
    stimuli = lexsync.match_stimuli(
        pool, {**design, "matching": {"method": method}}, schema
    )
    report = lexsync.match_report(
        stimuli, ["length", "n_density", "old20"], schema
    )
    print(method, [round(d, 3) for d in report["comparisons"]["cohens_d"]])

    standardised_euclidean [0.023, 0.04, 0.02]
joint [0.0, 0.0, 0.0]
mahalanobis [0.0, 0.023, 0.016]
optimal [0.0, 0.0, 0.0]

    The two pairwise methods reach exact equality on all three controls here, because a pool of this size contains, for most anchor words, a low-frequency counterpart with the same length, the same neighbourhood count and the same OLD20. The default and the covariance-aware method leave a standardised difference in the second decimal place. None of these differences would matter to a result. The default remains the default because it is byte-identical across engines and generalises past two conditions, and that is the trade lexsync makes: an algorithm is adopted as a default only if it can keep the guarantee.

    Reading the realised control¶

    match_report is what turns a selection into a claim you can defend. It returns a dictionary with two frames. descriptives gives n, mean, SD, minimum, median and maximum per condition per dimension. comparisons contrasts every pair of conditions on each dimension. The rows against the first condition, the anchor, come first, and every further pair follows in condition order, with the earlier condition of the pair named in reference. A 2 x 2 design such as en_andrews_repro therefore shows the contrasts within each factor, such as its two low-frequency cells, beside the three against its first cell.

    stimuli = lexsync.match_stimuli(pool, design, schema)
report = lexsync.match_report(
    stimuli, ["length", "frequency", "n_density", "old20"], schema
)
print(report["comparisons"].to_string(index=False))

    Each row carries the statistics below, each answering something the others cannot.

    cohens_d is the standardised mean difference, using the pooled standard deviation. It is the mean of the condition named in reference minus the mean of the condition in that row, so a positive d means the reference scores higher. A condition with a single item has no spread, so d and its interval are then left empty. d_ci_low and d_ci_high bound it with the 90% interval that goes with a two one-sided tests decision at the .05 level (Lakens, 2017), built from the large-sample standard error of d (Hedges & Olkin, 1985). That standard error grows with d, so the interval on the manipulated dimension is much wider than on a well-matched control. The interval is reported alongside the verdict because it keeps the dependence on item count visible: with few items it is wide, whatever the point estimate. Its upper limit is the largest imbalance still consistent with the stimuli you have. TOST tests the raw mean difference, so the interval is slightly wider than the one its decision implies, and for a limit close to the bound, tost_p is the verdict.

    tost_p and equivalent come from two one-sided tests against the schema's equivalence.bound_d, 0.5 by default, at equivalence.alpha. A non-significant t-test says the data failed to show a difference, which is not the same as showing there is none. TOST says the difference is smaller than the bound you declared uninteresting (Lakens, 2017). The bound of 0.5 is a convention and not a default of Lakens's, who asks for the bound to be justified for the study at hand, so set one you can defend. The verdict also depends on the number of items: at a bound of 0.5 and an alpha of .05, no dimension that varies can pass with fewer than 23 items per condition, even when the conditions are identical.

    Both statistics treat the selected items as a sample from a larger population, which they are not. Sassenhagen and Alday (2016) point out that a test on stimuli concerns a closed population (the items you chose). They recommend describing the stimuli, which the descriptives do, and entering potential confounds in the analysis as covariates.

    The datasheet's Methods paragraph follows that advice. It describes the realised control first: the largest difference on a matched dimension, in that dimension's unit, with d and its interval. The interval is labelled by the schema's alpha, 90% at .05 and 99% at .005. Raw differences in different units cannot be ranked against each other, so the largest is the one with the largest |d|. The two one-sided tests come second, as a check, with the number of matched comparisons they supported. When every condition holds fewer items than the bound needs, the paragraph says that no dimension that varies can pass, however well matched. It then names, largest first, up to five comparisons of a dimension the design did not control whose |d| exceeds the bound, such as the syllable difference in en_ndensity, or says that none does. The suggested model enters the matched dimensions as centred covariates.

    var_ratio is the condition's variance over the reference's. It is there because everything above is about means, and two conditions can share a mean while differing in spread, which still confounds (Armstrong et al., 2012; Austin, 2009). A ratio near 1 is balanced. A common heuristic treats anything outside roughly [0.5, 2] as unequal spread. It returns None when a variance is undefined.

    mean_diff is the same contrast in the dimension's own unit: the reference mean minus the condition mean, in letters, Zipf units, neighbours or syllables, to three places. It is defined whenever each condition has an item, so it remains when d is left empty.

    role says what the design did with the dimension in that pair. A dimension is manipulated when the two conditions' define_by windows on it differ, controlled when the design matches on it, and uncontrolled otherwise. The role belongs to the pair: in en_andrews_repro, frequency is manipulated between a high- and a low-frequency cell and uncontrolled between the two high-frequency cells, which share the window [5, 7]. run_pipeline derives the roles from the design and passes them to match_report as roles, a function of the row's condition, reference and dimension. Called without it, as above, match_report leaves role empty.

    The pipeline reports every dimension lexsync knows for the items, whether or not the design matches on it. Length, frequency, N, OLD20, syllables and bigram frequency come first, then the matched dimensions, every define_by dimension and every joined norm column, so a norm that defines the conditions appears as the manipulated dimension it is. Bigram frequency is computed for the report when the stimuli lack it, which leaves the stimuli file and the selection as they were. A dimension with no numeric value among the items, such as n_syllables for Chinese words, is left out. The uncontrolled rows are where a confound shows. In en_ndensity, the dense words have fewer estimated syllables than the sparse ones (d = -0.53, 90% CI [-0.79, -0.26]), which a report of the matched dimensions alone could not reveal.

    Two smaller functions round this out. describe_stimuli gives the descriptives alone, grouped by any column you like, condition included. balance_check reports columns whose value counts are unequal, which is how the pipeline notices an unbalanced condition and writes it into the run log.

    print(lexsync.balance_check(stimuli, "condition"))    # empty when balanced

    Continuous designs¶

    Splitting a continuous predictor into high and low conditions throws away information, costs power and can introduce selection artefacts (Kuperman, 2015; Liben-Nowell et al., 2019). A design can replace its conditions with a continuous block, and select a set that spreads over the predictor's ranks while windows trim the controls' tails, for analysis by regression or a mixed model.

    design = {
    "name": "continuous", "language": "english", "n_per_condition": 60,
    "continuous": {
        "predictor": "frequency",
        "controls": ["length", "n_density", "old20"]
    },
    "match_on": ["length", "n_density", "old20"],
    "matching": {
        "tolerance_k": {"length": 1.5, "n_density": 1.5, "old20": 1.5}
    },
}
pool = lexsync.build_pool(lexicon, {"length": [3, 8], "frequency": [3.8, 7.0]})
stimuli = lexsync.select_continuous_stimuli(pool, design, schema)
report = lexsync.match_report_continuous(
    stimuli, "frequency", ["length", "n_density", "old20"], schema
)
print(report["comparisons"].to_string(index=False))

    The selection is two deterministic passes, with no per-item matching and no random numbers. An even spread over the predictor's ranks across the whole pool sets a tolerance window on each control. The pool is then filtered to those windows, and a second even spread over the ranks of the filtered pool is the selection. Because both passes reuse the matcher's even-spread primitive, the two engines select byte-identical stimuli here as well.

    Because the spread is over ranks and not values, the selection follows the pool's distribution of the predictor. In the en_freqcontinuous demonstration its quantiles lie within 0.06 of the pool's, and 95% of the items fall in the lower half of the frequency range, since frequent words are rare in any lexicon. The windows trim the controls' tails without holding the controls constant.

    match_report_continuous returns the same {descriptives, comparisons} shape as match_report, so the pipeline and the datasheet stay uniform, but the comparisons describe a predictor. Its realised span replaces the effect size, and each control's Pearson correlation with the predictor replaces the equivalence verdict. Those correlations are what you report: at k = 1.5 the three controls sit between −0.248 and 0.069, and a control that stays correlated belongs in the model as a covariate. Tightening k does not reliably lower the correlations. In en_freqcontinuous, k = 1.0 gives r = 0.070 between frequency and n_density, against 0.301 at k = 1.5, but on the unscreened en.csv it gave 0.213 against −0.049. At k = 0.25 only 30 items fall within the windows, fewer than the 80 the design needs, so the selector relaxes them to the whole pool.

    Each row also gives the dimension's realised SD as sd. For the predictor, sd_ratio sets it against the SD of as many values spaced evenly over the same range. That SD is (max - min) * sqrt(n * (n + 1) / 12) / (n - 1), which approaches (max - min) / sqrt(12) as n grows, so an even spread has a ratio of 1 at any size. A ratio below 1 means the items bunch together more than an even spread would: en_freqcontinuous has 0.59, an SD of 0.53 against 0.90. A ratio above 1 means they gather towards the ends of the range. For a control, sd_ratio sets the realised SD against the SD of the pool the selector drew from, and window_low and window_high give the window its first pass set. In en_freqcontinuous, length keeps 0.76 of the pool's SD, so the window trims the tails of a control and leaves much of its spread. The windows come from the audit select_continuous_stimuli attaches to its result, which match_report_continuous reads unless it is given one as audit. A window that held too few items and was widened to the whole pool is recorded in the datasheet's window_relaxations. The datasheet's Methods paragraph sets the predictor's SD against that of an even spread, gives each control's window and its realised SD against the pool's, and says when the windows were widened.

    match_on must equal continuous.controls, the predictor must not appear among the controls, and there must be at least one control. All three are checked, and a design that breaks one of them raises.

    Items as a random factor¶

    A stimulus set is a sample from a language, not a fixed property of it, and analysing one set as if it were fixed over-generalises (Clark, 1973; Yarkoni, 2022). resample_stimuli produces several disjoint, independently matched sets from one pool, so a study can run a different sample per participant group, or show that an effect survives a change of items.

    pool = lexsync.build_pool(lexicon, {"length": [4, 8], "frequency": [3.5, 7.0]})
design = {
    "name": "resample", "language": "english", "n_per_condition": 20,
    "conditions": [
        {"name": "high_frequency", "define_by": {"frequency": [5.2, 7.0]}},
        {"name": "low_frequency", "define_by": {"frequency": [3.5, 4.4]}},
    ],
    "match_on": ["length", "n_density", "old20"],
}
stimuli = lexsync.resample_stimuli(pool, design, schema, n_sets=3)
print(stimuli["word"].nunique(), len(stimuli))

    120 120

    Each replicate is fully matched in its own right, drawn from the pool with every earlier replicate's items removed, and marked with a replicate column. No word is reused, which the equal counts above confirm. In a design file this is resample: {n_sets: 3}, and the counterbalancer then treats each replicate as an independent set and numbers trial order within it. The whole thing stays deterministic because the matcher is deterministic and the used-item set evolves identically in both engines.

    Expect this to exhaust a small pool. Three sets of 20 per condition need 120 distinct words that all satisfy their bands, and a pool that comfortably supports one set may raise on the third.

    Pseudowords¶

    Lexical decision needs non-words, and the usual difficulty is that they must be orthographically plausible without being real. build_lexdec_stimuli draws real words by an even spread across the byte-ordered pool, then generates a length-matched pseudoword for each.

    pool = lexsync.build_pool(lexicon, {"length": [4, 7], "frequency": [3.5, 6.0]})
stimuli = lexsync.build_lexdec_stimuli(
    pool, n=8, reference_words=lexicon["word"].tolist()
)
print(
    stimuli[["target", "condition", "length", "set"]]
    .head(3).to_string(index=False)
)

     target condition  length  set
abandon      word       7    1
 cannot      word       6    2
efforts      word       7    3

    The presented string is target, the conditions are word and pseudoword, and set pairs each word with its non-word twin. reference_words should be the full lexicon: it supplies the bigram statistics and the list of real forms a pseudoword must avoid, and defaults to the pool when omitted, which is rarely what you want.

    Two methods are available, chosen per design under items.generation.method.

    letter_substitution, the default, changes as few letters as possible subject to three constraints: every resulting bigram must be attested in the corpus, the form must not be in the reference list or already generated, and the length must be preserved exactly. Single substitutions are searched first, then pairs as a fallback. Among the legal candidates the most bigram-typical wins, with a byte-order tie-break.

    subsyllabic splits each word into onset, nucleus and coda constituents and swaps whole constituents for attested alternatives of the same role and length, so the pseudowords keep their syllabic structure. Codas and nuclei are changed before onsets, which carry the most identifying orthography. Up to ceil(2k/3) of a word's k constituents are replaced, each swap preserves length, and a word with no legal swap falls back to letter substitution, so every word yields a pseudoword. It is a deterministic orthographic approximation of Wuggy (Keuleers & Brysbaert, 2010), and it differs from Wuggy in more than the phonological model. Wuggy matches the template's transition frequencies, matches letter length by default and has orthographic modules for several languages, and by default it keeps two thirds of the segments where lexsync replaces up to two thirds. What lexsync gains is cross-engine reproducibility.

    subsyllabic = lexsync.build_lexdec_stimuli(
    pool, n=8, reference_words=lexicon["word"].tolist(), method="subsyllabic")

    In both methods, the scoring picks the most bigram-typical candidate and does not match the base word, so the pseudowords come out more bigram-typical than their words. The difference in mean bigram probability is about d = −1.0, words minus pseudowords, in en_lexdec and en_lexdec_wuggy alike. Read the generated forms before a study runs.

    Both generators process base words in byte order, so the set of already-used pseudowords evolves identically in R and Python, and both select byte-identical stimuli. Their alphabet is derived from the reference. It is the set of letters the reference words attest, in UTF-8 byte order, so a Spanish lexicon lends them á, é, í, ó, ú, ü and ñ, which one word in six carries in a typical Spanish band. build_lexdec_stimuli keeps the pool's words written wholly in those letters, and a generated design's datasheet counts these words in the band as its candidates. A word with a hyphen, an apostrophe or a digit is left out. segment_subsyllabic takes its nuclei from the vowels of the syllable estimate, whatever their accent, and a word without one, such as a Russian or Greek word, goes down the letter-substitution path. Chinese is refused, since Unicode counts its characters as letters but a form made by swapping one character for another is of unknown validity as a pseudoword. In the Spanish demonstration, design_es_lexdec.yaml, 8 of the 60 words and 4 of the pseudowords hold a letter outside a to z, and the pseudowords are checked against wordfreq's Spanish word list.

    The stimuli give each pseudoword its base word and its edit_distance from it, the Levenshtein distance, and give a word its own form and 0. A generated design's report compares the words with their pseudowords on length, which they share by construction, and on bigram frequency, N and OLD20, which the generator does not hold. The report marks length as controlled and the other three as uncontrolled, and it computes those three against the lexicon on a copy of the stimuli. Since the most bigram-plausible candidate wins, the pseudowords tend to be more bigram-typical than their words: in en_lexdec the difference is d = -0.97 (90% CI [-1.29, -0.65]).

    A pseudoword is a non-word only relative to the reference words, so a real word too rare for the lexicon can still come out as one. The bundled English and Spanish lexica stop at Zipf 2.95 and 3.11, and the English demonstrations produced crier and pall this way until they named the list below. exclude names further known word forms that no pseudoword may equal. They are trimmed, lower-cased and put in NFC like the lexicon's words, and they take no part in the bigram statistics. In a design the list goes under items.generation.exclude, and each entry is a file with one form per line, a CSV file with a word column, or a registry corpus already fetched into the cache:

    items:
  source: generate
  lexicon: corpora/derived/en_v2.csv
  generation:
    exclude: [corpora/derived/en_known_forms.txt]

    Each file must be UTF-8 text, and no form in a one-form-per-line file may hold a tab, a comma or a semicolon. Such a file is a table whose rows would each be kept whole as a form that matches nothing, so it is refused. The datasheet records each list with its checksum and the number of forms it holds, and the Methods paragraph names it. In the repository, python corpora/fetch_corpora.py --known-forms en es writes wordfreq's fuller lists to corpora/derived/ (289,023 English and 334,625 Spanish forms at Zipf 1.0 or above). They are not shipped in the package.

    A long list costs the pseudowords some of their form. The generators' only check on form is that each letter pair occurs somewhere in the lexicon, so with fewer candidates left the most bigram-typical one can be ill-formed. In the English demonstrations, 13 of the 140 pseudowords are ill-formed, among them fdes, gngs and ntena. Each lacks a vowel or opens or closes on a letter pair that opens or closes no screened English word of three letters or more with a vowel. Without the list, the same designs give 5 such pseudowords. Read the generated forms before a study runs.

    generate_pseudowords and make_pseudoword are available directly if you want the forms without the lexical-decision scaffolding around them.

    ["house", "table"], lexicon["word"].tolist()
