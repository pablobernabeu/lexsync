# Corpus attribution

Lexical corpora used by lexsync, with their sources, licences and retrieval
dates. The bundled derivatives come from wordfreq, whose data files are
redistributable under CC BY-SA 4.0, and the screening columns of `en_v2.csv`
from SUBTLEX-US, which openlexicon distributes under the same licence and which
also chose the rows of the packages' English example slices, so the derivatives
carry that licence too. See LICENSE-DATA for the full reasoning.

This file is regenerated by corpora/fetch_corpora.py. Edits outside the
hand-written region below will not survive the next run.

## Demonstration corpora (bundled)

- **English ('en')**, 30000 words, Zipf 2.95-7.73. Source: wordfreq (Speer, 2022; data CC BY-SA 4.0), retrieved 2026-06-08. N and OLD20 computed by lexsync. Superseded by `en_v2.csv` for new designs, and kept unchanged so that earlier designs select what they selected.
- **English, screened against SUBTLEX-US ('en_v2')**, 30000 words, Zipf 2.95-7.73. Source: wordfreq (Speer, 2022; data CC BY-SA 4.0) screened against SUBTLEX-US (Brysbaert & New, 2009; data CC BY-SA 4.0), retrieved 2026-10-09. 6565 forms marked `nonstandard`, and N and OLD20 computed by lexsync against the other 23435.
- **Spanish ('es')**, 30000 words, Zipf 3.11-7.81. Source: wordfreq (Speer, 2022; data CC BY-SA 4.0), retrieved 2026-06-08. N and OLD20 computed by lexsync.
- **Spanish, gender-tagged subset ('es_gender')**, 10185 words, Zipf 3.11-6.95. Source: wordfreq (Speer, 2022; data CC BY-SA 4.0), retrieved 2026-06-14. N and OLD20 computed by lexsync.
- **Chinese (Mandarin) ('zh')**, 20000 words, Zipf 3.31-6.56. Source: wordfreq (Speer, 2022; data CC BY-SA 4.0), retrieved 2026-06-10. N and OLD20 computed over characters by lexsync.

## Known-form lists (repository only)

Each list holds every form in wordfreq's large list for a language at Zipf 1.0
or above that the language's letters admit. `fetch_corpora.py --known-forms`
writes them for a design's `items.generation.exclude`, so that a generated
pseudoword cannot be a real word too rare for the lexicon. Neither package
ships them.

- **English ('en')**, 289023 forms in `en_known_forms.txt`, sha256 `31fa1bfbf347a94ca31dcdc0fe69be459b5829621b9b194469c725ab64e5e2b4`. Source: wordfreq's 'large' list at Zipf 1.0 or above (Speer, 2022; data CC BY-SA 4.0), retrieved 2026-10-03.
- **Spanish ('es')**, 334625 forms in `es_known_forms.txt`, sha256 `281fa6c374e1fa6550e6f5700d19b9bae4ccb664cfd3fc37ca6ffc087acdd776`. Source: wordfreq's 'large' list at Zipf 1.0 or above (Speer, 2022; data CC BY-SA 4.0), retrieved 2026-10-03.

## How the derivatives were built

The `en`, `es` and `zh` corpora above were built from wordfreq by
`corpora/fetch_corpora.py`, which takes the most frequent forms admitted by the
language's script, keeps those within the length bounds, and computes Coltheart's
N and OLD20 over the resulting set. The frequencies are wordfreq's own Zipf
values, and everything else is computed here and is inspectable in the file.

The `es_gender` corpus is one exception. It is a derivative of `es` rather than a
separate retrieval, restricted to words with canonical Spanish gender endings (-a
feminine, -o masculine, with gerunds excluded) and tagged with a `gender` column,
and it backs the demonstration that reproduces the gender-assignment design of
González Alonso et al. (2025). It is rebuilt by `corpora/build_es_gender.py`,
which reproduces the committed file byte for byte. Run that script whenever `es`
is rebuilt. A test in the Python suite fails if the two fall out of step.

Two approximations travel with it. Grammatical gender is read off the word ending,
where the original study looked it up in a lexical database, EsPal (Duchon et al.,
2013), so a word whose ending misrepresents its gender is mislabelled: `problema`
and `mapa` are masculine, `mano` and `foto` feminine, and all four are tagged the
wrong way here. Separately, the gerund exclusion tests the
ending -endo, which is broader than the gerund ending -iendo and so also removes
thirteen ordinary words, among them `estupendo`, `tremendo`, `atuendo` and
`aprendo`. Narrowing it would change which stimuli the demonstration selects, so
the behaviour is kept, and documented here so that nobody has to rediscover it.

## The screened English lexicon

`en_v2` is the other exception. `fetch_corpora.py --screen-en` builds it from
`en.csv`, whose 30,000 forms and frequencies it keeps, together with its N and
OLD20 as `n_density_raw` and `old20_raw`. Its second source, SUBTLEX-US
(Brysbaert & New, 2009), is thus a direct source of this file as well as one of
the subtitle lists wordfreq blends. The build reads the copy openlexicon
distributes under CC BY-SA 4.0, `SUBTLEXus74286wordstextversion.tsv` from
https://lexique.org/databases/SUBTLEX-US/ (3,298,971 bytes, SHA-256
`c5f86f065fc5d057fbf366433b8c5ca550aa7c24e128362dea4394f2b29c86e4`). It refuses a
copy with other bytes, and keeps its download in the uncommitted `corpora/raw/`.

Four columns come from SUBTLEX-US. `in_subtlex_us` is 1 when it lists the form.
`lowercase_share` is the share of the form's uses that begin with a lower-case
letter (FREQlow over FREQcount, to three places), and is empty for a form
SUBTLEX-US lacks. `nonstandard` is 1 for a form SUBTLEX-US lacks or whose share is
below 0.10. It marks 6,565 of the 30,000 forms, 3,875 of them absent from SUBTLEX-US
and 2,690 rarely written in lower case, mostly names and acronyms.
`reference_member` is 1 for the other 23,435, and `n_density` and `old20` count
neighbours among those only, so the marked forms no longer count as neighbours.
`cat` has 21 neighbours, where `en.csv` gives it 32.

The threshold is low because a capital at the start of a sentence pulls ordinary
words down. Even what, how, why, maybe, okay and yes begin with a lower-case
letter in under half their uses. The rule still marks the days of the week, the
months other than march and may, most nationality words, god, ok and tv. It also
marks every form the 2009 corpus lacks, such as selfie, emoji and covid. A
design that needs such forms can leave `nonstandard` unfiltered and remove what
it does not want with `exclude_words`. The build has an allow-list for forms to
keep whatever the rule says, and it is empty. A form added to it will be listed
here with the reason.

The screen covers English only. No source of letter case or of known words with a
verified licence exists for Spanish or Chinese. `es`, `es_gender` and `zh`
therefore still hold names, acronyms, loans and other non-standard forms, and
their N and OLD20 count those forms as neighbours. The known-form lists above do
not fill that gap, since they come from wordfreq itself and hold the same kinds of
forms.

## What the derived lexica contain

The `en`, `es` and `zh` lexica hold the most frequent forms wordfreq returns for
each script (for English, every form that matches `^[a-z]+$` after
lower-casing), with no screening for lexical status. They therefore include
proper names (aaron, dubai), acronyms and abbreviations (bbc, atm, aclu),
interjections and internet forms (lmao, alot) and loanwords. The Spanish lexicon
also holds English words (blue, chat) and spellings that drop an accent (ano for
año). Coltheart's N and OLD20 are computed over all of these forms, so a name or
an acronym counts as a neighbour like any word.

Such forms are not spread evenly over frequency bands, so they can confound a
frequency manipulation. When the `en_andrews_repro` demonstration read `en.csv`,
24 of its 36 low-frequency items were forms that the screen above marks as
non-standard, against none of its 36 high-frequency items. A design on `en.csv`,
`es.csv` or `zh.csv` should therefore have its selection read before a study
runs, and what is found removed with `exclude_words`. For English, `en_v2.csv`
marks those forms itself.

## The demonstrations and the example slices

The English demonstrations select from `en_v2.csv` with
`pool_filters: {nonstandard: [0, 0]}`. The screen does not catch every unsuitable
form, since subtitles write some abbreviations in lower case often enough to pass
it (mph, msg, yrs), and it was never meant to catch taboo words. The
demonstrations therefore also leave out, through `exclude_words`, the words of
`config/exclude_en_demo.txt`. That list was made by reading what the
demonstrations selected until they selected none of its forms. Its header states
the criteria. A form is listed when it is offensive, taboo or distressing. It is
also listed when it is not a standard English word, such as an abbreviation, a
word of another language, a proper name or a spelling without its apostrophe.
The generated lexical-decision demonstrations also check their pseudowords
against `en_known_forms.txt`. The English example slices both packages bundle
hold 3,000 of the forms `en_v2.csv` keeps, with its frequencies, N and OLD20,
and `fetch_corpora.py --slice-en` rebuilds them from it.

## Data currency and reproducibility

The bundled derived corpora are a fixed, dated, checksummed snapshot, so the
demonstrations reproduce exactly with no network access or corpus download. The
optional `wordfreq` connector, used only to fetch further languages, draws on
wordfreq's frozen data: the package was retired in September 2024 and is no
longer updated, because generative-AI text had begun to distort web-derived word
frequencies (Speer, 2024, `SUNSET.md`). For lexsync this is a strength, since
wordfreq 3.x is a stable snapshot of human language usage through roughly 2021.
A fetch today returns the same frequencies as a fetch next year, and every
derived corpus records its retrieval date and checksum. lexsync pins the
connector to the 3.x line for this reason.

One caveat travels with the Mandarin Chinese corpus. Among the sources wordfreq
blends for Chinese is a wordlist shipped with the Jieba segmenter whose
provenance wordfreq's own documentation describes as unknown, so the Chinese
frequencies rest on a slightly less traceable footing than the others.
